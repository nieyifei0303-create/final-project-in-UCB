The problem

Recyclable on paper, unseparable in the hand.

A conventional perfume package combines glass, plastic, metal, adhesives, coatings, films and board. Each material may be recyclable alone. Together, they arrive as one object that neither the buyer nor the local recycling stream can take apart.

What a single purchase delivers today
  • Heavy glass bottle Kept as display, rarely refilled
  • Crimped metal collar Bonded to the neck
  • Pump, spring and dip tube Mixed plastic and metal
  • Decorative film and coating Contaminates the glass stream
  • Rigid presentation box Laminated board
  • Internal plastic support Not sorted at home
  • Labels, seals, cord, magnets Adhesive-bonded
The real issue

The problem is not that there is too much packaging. It is that the whole package is produced, sold and discarded again with every purchase, while the buyer has no reason and no route to keep the bottle.

So the design move is not to swap in one greener material. It is to make the bottle worth keeping, easy to maintain, and simple to hand back.

The bottle

Designed for the tenth fill, not the first.

Four decisions turn a display object into a serviceable one. Use the diagram at the top of the page to see how they come apart.

Body

Kept, not returned

Lightweight glass with recycled content, sized to survive many cycles. It stays the visual heart of the product so the owner wants to hold on to it.

Pump

A module, not a puzzle

The pump screws off as one complete unit. Nobody is asked to separate springs from tubes at home — the whole module goes back to the store, which handles disassembly.

Opening

A standard refill fitting

Perfume is replenished without breaking a seal or damaging the bottle. The fitting limits spillage, contamination and overfilling during in-store service.

Wear parts

Replace the part, keep the object

Only components that wear out — the pump, the cap — are swapped. Keep, refill, repair, return: maintenance replaces repurchase.

First purchase

One fibre, one stream, one time.

Glass still needs protection in transit, so the packaging is not simply deleted. It is reduced to a single fibre family that a household already knows how to sort — and it is not reproduced at every refill.

Mono-material by rule

  • No plastic lamination
  • No magnetic closures
  • No metallic decorative film
  • Minimal or no adhesive
  • Water-based printing
  • Folded fibre inserts, not mixed-material supports

Inner and outer match

The protective insert uses the same fibre as the outer package, so there is nothing to pull apart before recycling. One box, one bin, no decision.

In-store refills include no new presentation box. The luxury package is bought once, not re-bought every cycle.

The neck label earns its place

A detachable paper label ships only with the first bottle, and only because it does three jobs: tamper evidence, required product information, and first access to the digital passport. It is held by a folded locking structure — no glue, no cord, no plastic clip.

Digital product passport

The connector, not an extra webpage.

The innovation

Two loops, one code.

Nothing here depends on inventing a new material. The contribution is the integration: packaging design, refill service, digital verification, incentives and restoration held together by a single permanent identifier.

Material

Packaging loop

Keep→ Refill→ Maintain→ Return→ Recover

Physical parts move between the owner and the store, and only the store handles disassembly.

Behaviour

Engagement loop

Scan→ Understand→ Refill→ Be rewarded→ Restore→ Follow

The passport supplies the evidence that the bottle was genuinely reused and the contribution genuinely released.

Project boundary

What we design, and what we leave alone.

Holding this line keeps the project on packaging and the service system around it — which is where the brief and the assessment sit.

In scope

  • Primary bottle design
  • Removable pump and cap
  • First-purchase protective packaging
  • In-store refill service
  • Digital product passport
  • Consumer incentives
  • Ecological restoration participation

Out of scope

  • Fragrance formulation
  • Scent personalisation
  • Ingredient extraction technology
  • Perfume manufacturing emissions
  • Company-wide sustainability strategy
Research question

How might we redesign perfume packaging so the primary bottle stays in use across many refill cycles, while a digital passport makes reuse, component return and ecological restoration visible and rewarding?
